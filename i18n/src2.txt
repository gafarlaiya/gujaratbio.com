1	Micronutrients in agriculture: what they are, how they work and why they matter
2	Micronutrients and trace elements for farmers: the full list, what each does, deficiency signs, fertilizer types, and how to diagnose and correct problems.
3	Micronutrients are the nutrients a crop needs in very small amounts: zinc, iron, manganese, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine and nickel. They switch on the enzymes, hormones and processes that let the crop use nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, form chlorophyll, flower, set fruit and resist stress. A shortage of any one limits the whole crop, however much NPK is applied, and Indian soil tests show such shortages are widespread.

14	What are micronutrients?
15	Plants need 17 essential nutrients. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come from air and water. The rest come from the soil and fall into groups by how much the crop needs. Micronutrients, also called trace elements, are needed in grams per acre rather than kilograms, but a crop cannot complete its life without them.

31	How micronutrients work
32	They switch on enzymes: zinc, manganese, copper, iron, molybdenum and nickel are parts of, or activators for, enzymes that drive growth and energy.
33	They make chlorophyll possible: iron and manganese are needed to form it and run photosynthesis; magnesium sits at its centre.
34	They make hormones: zinc is needed for auxin, the growth hormone.
35	They build cell walls: boron and calcium hold walls together, and silicon strengthens them.
36	They make flowers fertile: boron, zinc and copper are needed for pollen and seed set.
37	They help use nitrogen: molybdenum, nickel and sulphur turn nitrogen into protein; molybdenum and cobalt help legumes fix it.
38	They defend the crop: copper, manganese, boron and silicon strengthen tissue against pests and disease.

86	India's soils: shortages are widespread
87	An ICAR survey of 2,42,827 soil samples from 615 districts found 58.6% of soils short of sulphur, 51.2% of zinc, 44.7% of boron, 19.2% of iron, 17.4% of manganese and 11.4% of copper, counting hidden shortages. Many fields were short of two or three nutrients together, most often sulphur with zinc, zinc with boron and sulphur with boron. Alkaline and calcareous soils, low organic matter and years of urea, DAP and MOP without sulphur or micronutrients are the main reasons.
88	Indian soil figures: Shukla AK, Behera SK and others (2021). Deficiency of phyto-available sulphur, zinc, boron, iron, copper and manganese in soils of India. <em>Scientific Reports</em> 11, 19760. Based on 2,42,827 soil samples from 615 districts in 28 states. <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-99040-2" rel="noopener">Read the study</a>. The figures include soils with a hidden (latent) shortage.
89	Common practices that miss the cause
90	Applying more urea when leaves turn yellow, though yellowing from sulphur, iron, zinc or magnesium does not respond to nitrogen.
91	Using NPK water-soluble fertilizer as a cure-all, though most grades carry little or no micronutrient.
92	Spraying a pesticide when the crop looks weak, even when no pest is present.
93	Treating the nutrient that shows the symptom, when the real limit is another nutrient, the soil pH or the water supply.

194	What are micronutrients in agriculture?
195	Nutrients a crop needs in very small amounts: zinc, iron, manganese, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine and nickel. Calcium, magnesium and sulphur are secondary nutrients, and silicon and cobalt are beneficial elements.
196	What is the difference between micronutrients and trace elements?
197	In farming the two terms are used for the same group: nutrients needed only in trace amounts. Trace elements is sometimes also used for beneficial elements such as cobalt and selenium.
198	Which micronutrient is most deficient in Indian soils?
199	Among micronutrients, zinc, followed by boron. Sulphur, a secondary nutrient, is even more widely short.
200	Can NPK alone give a good crop?
201	Only if the soil supplies enough of every other nutrient. Where a micronutrient is short, more NPK does not lift the yield.
202	How do I know which micronutrient my crop needs?
203	Read the leaf symptoms, rule out pests and disease, and confirm with a soil and leaf test.

206	Micronutrient deficiency symptoms: how to read the leaves
207	Start with where the symptom appears. Nutrients that move in the plant (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, molybdenum) show on old leaves first. Nutrients that do not move (iron, zinc, manganese, copper, boron, calcium, sulphur) show on young leaves, tips, flowers or fruit. Then look at the pattern and colour, rule out pests, disease, water and weather, and confirm with a soil or leaf test.

244	Step 3: rule out the look-alikes
245	Sucking pests and mites: check the underside of leaves with a hand lens.
246	Viruses: mosaic patterns, curling and stunting that spread plant to plant.
247	Fungal and bacterial disease: spots with rings or margins, rotting, a spreading pattern.
248	Herbicide drift: sudden damage along the field edge or in the wind direction.
249	Waterlogging, drought, cold and heat: the whole field changes after the weather does.
250	Root damage from nematodes, termites or root rot: lift a plant and check the roots.
251	Step 4: confirm before you spend
252	A soil test shows what the soil can supply. The Soil Health Card test covers zinc, iron, manganese, copper, boron and sulphur.
253	A leaf test shows what the crop actually took up.
254	A strip test: spray one nutrient on a few rows and compare the new growth after one to two weeks.
255	Keep photos with dates so you can compare later.
256	Taking a useful photo for advice
257	Photograph a whole plant, then a close-up of the worst leaf, both sides.
258	Show the youngest and the oldest leaves.
259	Add a photo of the field to show the pattern.

270	Zinc (Zn) in crops: role, deficiency signs and how to correct it
271	What zinc does in crops, how a shortage looks in each crop, why it happens, what it costs if ignored, and how growers confirm and correct it.
272	Zinc is a micronutrient the crop needs for its growth hormone (auxin), for more than a hundred enzymes, for chlorophyll and for healthy pollen. A zinc-short crop has small, narrow, pale new leaves, short internodes and poor tillering or flowering. Zinc is the most common micronutrient shortage in Indian soils, especially in alkaline, calcareous and low organic matter fields.

283	What zinc does in the plant
284	Helps the plant make auxin, the hormone that drives cell growth and normal leaf size.
285	Switches on many enzymes for energy, protein building and sugar use.
286	Needed for chlorophyll formation, so the leaf stays green.
287	Keeps cell membranes healthy, which helps roots hold nutrients and resist stress.
288	Supports pollen and seed formation, so it matters at flowering and grain fill.
289	How zinc moves in the plant
290	Zinc moves only a little inside the plant, so a shortage shows first on young and middle leaves. Once a leaf is formed short of zinc, it does not recover; only the new growth after correction looks normal.
291	Signs of zinc deficiency
292	Pale or yellow patches between the veins of young leaves, while veins stay green.
293	Small, narrow leaves, often bunched at the tip (little leaf or rosetting).
294	Short internodes, so the plant looks stunted and bushy.
295	Rusty brown spots on older leaves of paddy a few weeks after transplanting.
296	A white or pale band on both sides of the midrib in young maize leaves.
297	Delayed flowering and maturity, poor grain filling.
298	Crop by crop
299	What a shortage looks like
300	Khaira disease: rusty brown spots and stunting two to four weeks after transplanting, uneven patches in the field.
301	White bud: a broad pale band on each side of the midrib on young leaves, short plants.
302	Wheat
303	Grey-green or pale patches in the middle of leaves, uneven growth, poor tillering.
304	Mottle leaf: yellow patches between green veins, small pointed leaves, rosettes at shoot tips.
305	Small thick leaves, bronzing and short internodes.
306	More crops and look-alikes: <a href="/micronutrients/zinc/zinc-deficiency-symptoms-in-crops">Zinc deficiency symptoms crop by crop</a>.
307	Where and why it happens
308	In India, an ICAR survey of more than two lakh soil samples found 51.2% of soils short of zinc, counting hidden shortages.
309	Alkaline and calcareous soils, common in Saurashtra and much of Gujarat.
310	Soils low in organic matter.
311	Fields where the topsoil was removed in land levelling.
312	Heavy, repeated use of phosphate fertilizers such as DAP.
313	Flooded paddy soils.
314	Cold, wet weather early in the season, which slows root uptake.
315	Sandy soils that are low in zinc to start with.
316	How it works with other nutrients
317	High phosphorus can lower zinc uptake, so DAP-heavy fields often show zinc shortage.
318	In high pH and calcareous soils zinc becomes less available.
319	Very high zinc can lower iron uptake.
320	Nitrogen raises the crop's need for zinc, so a well-fed crop shows zinc shortage sooner.
321	See <a href="/micronutrients/nutrient-interactions">nutrient interactions</a>.
322	What happens if it is not managed
323	Stunted plants and fewer tillers or branches.
324	Late and weak flowering, poor fruit and grain set.
325	Lower grain weight and a poorer harvest even when NPK was applied.
326	Grain and fodder with less zinc, which matters for people and animals who eat them.
327	See <a href="/micronutrients/consequences-of-micronutrient-deficiency">the full picture of what unmanaged shortages cost</a>.
328	How to confirm it
329	Look at the youngest leaves first: small, narrow, pale leaves point to zinc.
330	Rule out pests, virus and herbicide drift, which can look similar.
331	Ask for a DTPA zinc soil test, which is part of the Soil Health Card test.
332	A leaf test at the right stage confirms what the crop actually took up.
333	Spray a small strip and compare the new growth after one to two weeks.
334	How to correct it
335	Soil application of zinc sulphate before or at sowing in deficient fields. Do not mix it with phosphate fertilizers in the same bag or tank.
336	Foliar spray of zinc sulphate or a chelated zinc on the standing crop for quick correction of new growth.
337	Zinc-coated urea or zincated fertilizers where available.
338	Seed or seedling root dip for crops where your agriculture department advises it.
339	Zinc solubilising bacteria and organic manure to free zinc already locked in the soil and keep it available.
340	Take the dose from your soil test report, the product label or your agriculture officer.
341	Too much zinc
342	Zinc excess is rare in fields. It can happen near industrial waste or after repeated heavy doses.
343	Too much zinc can cause iron shortage, seen as yellow young leaves.

1098	Cobalt is a beneficial element. The plant itself is not shown to need it, but the Rhizobium bacteria in legume nodules do: they need cobalt to make vitamin B12, without which they cannot fix nitrogen.
