16	Leaves Turning Yellow Even After Fertilizer: Causes and Fixes
17	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Why leaves turn yellow even after you apply...
18	Why leaves turn yellow even after you apply fertilizer, and how to find the real cause
19	Another bag of urea is the most common answer to yellow leaves, and often the wrong one. If the roots are drowning, or the soil is locking iron, the crop stays yellow while you pay for fertilizer it cannot use.
20	If leaves stay yellow after fertilizer, the crop is usually not short of that fertilizer. Either the roots cannot take it up (waterlogging, dry soil, root disease, salt, high pH), or the shortage is a different nutrient such as magnesium, sulphur, iron or zinc. Which leaves turn yellow first tells you where to look.
21	Step 1: which leaves are yellow?
22	Likely shortage
23	Old, lower leaves, whole leaf pale
24	Common after heavy rain or waterlogging, which washes away or locks nitrogen
25	Old leaves, yellow between green veins
26	Often on sandy soils, or where much potash is used
27	Old leaves, yellow or burnt edges
28	Potash
29	Edges dry first, then the whole leaf
30	Young, top leaves, whole leaf pale
31	Looks like nitrogen shortage but on new leaves
32	Young leaves, yellow between green veins
33	Iron or manganese
34	Very common on high pH, calcareous soils of Gujarat
35	Young leaves small, crowded, mottled
36	Short internodes, small narrow leaves
37	Patches in the field, plants wilt at midday
38	Root rot, nematodes, termites or waterlogging; dig a plant
39	These patterns are a guide. Several shortages can show together, and a leaf or soil test confirms them. See <a href="/micronutrients/deficiency-symptoms">deficiency symptoms with photos</a>.
40	Step 2: check the roots before the bag
41	Pull out one yellow plant and one green plant. Compare the roots: white and many is healthy; brown, few, rotten or knotted is a root problem.
42	Waterlogged soil for even two or three days suffocates roots. Fertilizer cannot help until the soil drains.
43	Very dry soil also stops uptake. Fertilizer on dry soil sits unused.
44	Salty water or soil makes leaves yellow and burnt at the edges; see <a href="/guides/salty-borewell-water-for-crops">salty borewell water</a>.
45	Cold nights in winter can make leaves pale for a while; they recover as days warm.
46	Step 3: is the soil locking nutrients?
47	On soils with pH above about 8, iron, zinc and manganese are present but locked. Adding more to the soil often fails; foliar sprays or chelated forms work better.
48	Too much phosphorus can lock zinc. Too much potash can lock magnesium.
49	Low organic matter means nutrients wash away or get locked quickly. See <a href="/guides/compost-and-humic-acid">compost and humic acid</a>.
50	Fix drainage or irrigation first, if roots are the problem.
51	Get a soil test, and a leaf test if you can, before adding more NPK.
52	Correct the specific shortage the pattern and test show, at the label dose.
53	Keep roots strong with organic matter; a healthy root system takes up more of what is already there.
54	Ask us with a photo of the whole plant, a close-up of the leaf, and the roots.
55	<a href="https://iiss.icar.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ICAR-Indian Institute of Soil Science</a>
56	Why are leaves yellow even after applying urea?
57	The crop may not be short of nitrogen. Waterlogging, dry soil, root damage, salt, high pH or a shortage of magnesium, sulphur, iron or zinc can all cause yellowing that urea does not fix.
58	Which deficiency causes yellow leaves between green veins?
59	On old leaves, usually magnesium. On young leaves, usually iron or manganese, common on high pH soils.
60	Can too much water make leaves yellow?
61	Yes. Waterlogged roots cannot breathe or take up nutrients, so leaves turn yellow even with plenty of fertilizer.
62	Should I spray or add more fertilizer to the soil?
63	Find the cause first. Locked micronutrients on high pH soil respond better to foliar sprays; root and water problems need fixing before any fertilizer works.

64	Why Flowers Drop Before Fruit Set, and What Helps
65	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Why flowers drop before fruit set in...
66	Why flowers drop before fruit set in vegetables, cotton and fruit crops, and what helps
67	A field full of flowers is not a field full of fruit. Heat, thirst, too much urea or a few thrips inside the flowers can empty the plant in a week, and the spray most farmers reach for fixes none of them.
68	Every crop sheds some flowers; that is normal. Heavy flower drop is usually caused by heat or cold, a dry or waterlogged root zone, too much nitrogen, sucking pests such as thrips and mites, poor pollination, or a shortage of boron. Find which one applies before you spray anything.
69	Normal drop and problem drop
70	A plant makes more flowers than it can carry. Cotton sheds many of its squares and young bolls, and a mango panicle keeps only a few fruits out of hundreds of flowers. It becomes a problem when the drop is sudden, most flowers fall, or the plant keeps flowering and setting nothing.
71	The common causes
72	What happens
73	What helps
74	Very hot days and hot winds dry pollen; tomato and chilli drop flowers in heat waves
75	Irrigate in the evening, mulch, use shade nets in nurseries
76	Cold and fog
77	Pollen does not work well on cold nights; mango and vegetables set poorly in long foggy spells
78	Wait for warmer days; protect flowers from mildew
79	Water stress
80	Dry spells, or a sudden flood after a dry spell
81	Light, regular irrigation; drip where possible
82	Too much nitrogen
83	The plant grows leaves instead of holding flowers
84	Cut nitrogen at flowering; balance with potash
85	Thrips, mites, hoppers
86	They feed on flower parts and buds
87	Check the inside of flowers with a white paper; act early
88	Poor pollination
89	Few bees and insects, often after sprays at bloom
90	Do not spray open flowers; spray morning or evening
91	Boron shortage
92	Pollen tubes do not grow; flowers fall or fruit is misshapen
93	Correct only if a soil or leaf test, or clear symptoms, show it
94	Crop notes
95	<strong>Tomato, chilli, brinjal:</strong> heat, thrips and mites, and too much nitrogen are the usual causes. See <a href="/crops/chilli">chilli</a> and <a href="/crops/vegetables">vegetables</a>.
96	<strong>Cotton:</strong> squares and young bolls fall with water stress, cloudy wet spells, excess nitrogen and sucking pests. See <a href="/crops/cotton">cotton</a>.
97	<strong>Pomegranate:</strong> male flowers always fall; that is normal. Watch for heat and irregular water. See <a href="/crops/pomegranate">pomegranate</a>.
98	<strong>Mango:</strong> see <a href="/crop-problems/mango-fruit-drop">mango flower and fruit drop</a> and <a href="/guides/mango-flowering-care">mango flowering care</a>.
99	Before you spray
100	Look at the dropped flowers: a clean break with no damage points to stress; brown, chewed or scarred flowers point to pests or disease.
101	Check soil moisture with your hand at root depth.
102	Think back over the last week: heat wave, cold spell, heavy rain, a big urea dose or a spray at bloom?
103	Growth regulators are sold for flower drop. Use them only on your state university's or KVK's advice, at the label dose.
104	Seaweed and amino acid based products are used before and during flowering to help plants cope with heat and cold stress. They support a crop that is watered and fed well; they do not fix a pest or water problem. See <a href="/guides/what-are-biostimulants">what biostimulants are</a> and <a href="/micronutrients/boron">boron</a>.
105	<a href="https://ncipm.icar.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ICAR-National Centre for Integrated Pest Management</a>
106	Why are my flowers dropping before fruit set?
107	Usually heat or cold, water stress, too much nitrogen, sucking pests like thrips and mites, poor pollination or boron shortage. Some flower drop is normal in every crop.
108	Why do tomato and chilli flowers fall?
109	Most often heat waves and hot winds, thrips and mites, irregular water or too much nitrogen.
110	Does boron stop flower drop?
111	Only where boron is short. Boron helps pollen grow and fruit set. Adding it where it is not short does not help, and too much is harmful.
112	Should I spray a hormone to stop flower drop?
113	Only on your state university's or KVK's advice, at the label dose, after you have ruled out water, heat and pests.
### /guides/hard-compact-soil
114	Hard, Compact Soil: Why It Happens and How to Fix It
115	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Why soil becomes hard, compact and less...
116	Why soil becomes hard, compact and less productive, and how to bring it back
117	Your crop's roots may be stopping at the same depth your plough stops. Below that line sits a hard layer that no fertilizer can soften, and every season of ploughing at the same depth makes it harder.
118	Soil turns hard when organic matter falls, when it is ploughed or driven on while wet, when the same depth is tilled every year (a plough pan), or when sodium builds up from irrigation water. Roots then stay shallow, water runs off or stands, and yields fall. Organic matter, the right tillage at the right moisture, and gypsum where sodium is the cause bring it back.
119	Why soil becomes hard
120	Organic matter has fallen. Organic matter is the glue that keeps soil crumbly.
121	Ploughing or running a tractor on wet soil smears and packs it.
122	Tilling to the same depth every year forms a hard layer, a plough pan, just below.
123	Bare soil under heavy rain or flood irrigation forms a crust on top.
124	Sodium from irrigation water makes clay soils sticky when wet and hard as stone when dry.
125	Check your soil in 10 minutes
126	Push a steel rod into moist soil. If it stops suddenly at 15 to 25 cm, there is a plough pan.
127	Dig a pit 45 cm deep. Roots turning sideways at one level show the hard layer.
128	Drop a dry clod in a glass of water. A healthy clod holds together; a clod that melts into mud has poor structure; cloudy water that does not settle can point to sodium.
129	Watch after irrigation: water standing for a day, or running off, shows poor infiltration.
130	A soil test with pH, EC and, where needed, exchangeable sodium confirms a sodic soil. See <a href="/guides/how-to-take-a-soil-sample">how to take a soil sample</a>.
131	How to loosen it
132	Break a plough pan once with a chisel plough or subsoiler, when the soil is dry enough to crack, not wet.
133	Then keep it open: grow deep-rooted crops, green manure such as dhaincha or sunhemp, and return crop residues.
134	Add compost or farmyard manure every season. See <a href="/guides/compost-and-humic-acid">compost and humic acid</a>.
135	Do not plough or drive on wet soil.
136	Mulch or keep the soil covered, so rain does not crust it.
137	Use gypsum only where a soil or water test shows sodium is the problem, on a lab's advice.
138	<a href="/guides/why-yields-fall-on-old-land">Why yields fall on old land</a>, <a href="/guides/soil-microbial-activity">soil microbial activity</a> and <a href="/knowledge/soil-health">soil health</a>.
139	<a href="https://cssri.res.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ICAR-Central Soil Salinity Research Institute (sodic soils)</a>
140	Why is my soil becoming hard?
141	Usually falling organic matter, ploughing or driving on wet soil, a plough pan from tilling the same depth every year, crusting from heavy rain, or sodium from irrigation water.
142	How do I know if I have a plough pan?
143	Push a steel rod into moist soil. If it stops suddenly at about 15 to 25 cm, or roots turn sideways at one level in a pit, there is a hard layer.
144	Does gypsum soften hard soil?
145	Only where sodium causes the hardness. On ordinary compact soil, organic matter and correct tillage help; gypsum does not.
146	How long does it take to improve compact soil?
147	Breaking a plough pan works at once, but the soil packs again unless organic matter and good practice keep it open. Real improvement takes a few seasons.
### /guides/soil-microbial-activity
148	How to Improve Soil Microbial Activity and Beneficial Bacteria
149	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / How to improve soil microbial activity
150	How to improve soil microbial activity: feeding the life that feeds your crop
151	A handful of healthy soil holds more living organisms than there are people in India. They feed your crop every day, and they go hungry the moment the field is burnt, left bare or starved of organic matter.
152	Soil microbes need food (organic matter and living roots), water, air and cover. Feed them with compost, crop residues, green manure and living roots in the soil for as much of the year as possible. Avoid burning, bare soil, waterlogging and heavy tillage. Biofertilizers add useful microbes, but they survive only in a soil that feeds them.
153	What microbes do for your crop
154	Break down crop residues and manure into nutrients plants can use.
155	Free phosphorus, potash and zinc locked in the soil.
156	Fix nitrogen from the air, in legume roots and around other crops.
157	Make the glue that holds soil in crumbs, so water and roots move freely.
158	Compete with, and keep down, some disease-causing fungi.
159	What feeds them
160	Organic matter: compost, farmyard manure, crop residues. See <a href="/guides/how-to-start-composting">how to start composting</a>.
161	Living roots: roots release sugars that feed microbes. Avoid long bare fallows.
162	Variety: rotations and intercrops feed a wider range of microbes than one crop every year.
163	Moisture and air together: moist, crumbly soil, not dry dust and not waterlogged mud.
164	What harms them
165	Burning residues, which removes their food and heats the surface.
166	Bare soil in summer sun.
167	Waterlogging, which drives out air.
168	Frequent deep tillage, which breaks fungal networks and burns organic matter.
169	Seed or soil treated with chemicals at the same time as live biofertilizers; apply them separately, as the label says.
170	Signs of a living soil
171	Earthworms and their casts after rain.
172	A sweet, earthy smell when you dig.
173	Crumbly soil that holds together in a ball but breaks easily.
174	Crop residues that disappear within a season instead of lying for a year.
175	Water that soaks in quickly after irrigation.
176	Where biofertilizers fit
177	Biofertilizers add specific useful microbes, such as nitrogen fixers or phosphate solubilisers. They work best in soil with organic matter and moisture. See <a href="/guides/which-biofertilizers-to-use">which biofertilizers to use</a> and <a href="/products/immunity-consortia">Immunity Consortia</a>.
178	How can I increase beneficial bacteria in my soil?
179	Add compost and crop residues, keep living roots in the soil, rotate crops, avoid burning and waterlogging, and use biofertilizers in soil that has organic matter.
180	Do chemical fertilizers kill soil microbes?
181	Balanced fertilizer at normal doses does not kill soil life. Long-term use with no organic matter starves it. Some chemical seed treatments and fungicides can harm live biofertilizers applied at the same time.
182	How do I know my soil is biologically active?
183	Earthworms, an earthy smell, crumbly structure, residues that break down within a season, and water that soaks in quickly.
### /guides/which-biofertilizers-to-use
184	Which Biofertilizers to Use: A Crop-by-Crop Guide
185	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Which biofertilizers to use for your crop, and...
186	Which biofertilizers to use for your crop, and how to make them work
187	A biofertilizer is alive. Leave it in a hot shop or mix it with fungicide-treated seed, and you are paying for a packet of dead microbes.
188	Choose biofertilizers by the job: Rhizobium for pulses and groundnut, Azotobacter or Azospirillum for cereals, vegetables and cotton, Acetobacter for sugarcane, PSB for phosphorus, KMB for potash, ZSB for zinc, and mycorrhiza for roots. They are living products, so freshness, storage and soil organic matter decide whether they work.
189	What each one does
190	Biofertilizer
191	What it does
192	<a href="/products/immunity-rhizobium">Rhizobium</a>
193	Fixes nitrogen in root nodules
194	Gram, tur, moong, urad, soybean, groundnut
195	<a href="/products/immunity-azotobacter">Azotobacter</a>
196	Fixes nitrogen around roots
197	Wheat, cotton, vegetables, many non-legumes
198	<a href="/products/immunity-azospirillum">Azospirillum</a>
199	Fixes nitrogen, supports root growth
200	Maize, sorghum, millets, paddy
201	<a href="/products/immunity-acetobacter">Acetobacter</a>
202	Fixes nitrogen inside the plant
203	<a href="/products/immunity-psb">PSB</a> and <a href="/products/immunity-psf">PSF</a>
204	Free locked phosphorus
205	<a href="/products/immunity-kmb">KMB</a>
206	Free potash from soil minerals
207	<a href="/products/immunity-zsb">ZSB</a>
208	Free locked zinc
209	All crops, especially on high pH soils
210	<a href="/products/immunity-mycorrhiza">Mycorrhiza</a>
211	Extends roots to reach water and phosphorus
212	Fruit trees, vegetables, many field crops
213	<a href="/products/immunity-consortia">Consortia</a>
214	Several of the above together
215	Where one product is easier than many
216	How to apply them
217	Seed treatment: coat seed just before sowing and sow within a few hours.
218	Seedling dip: dip roots of transplants before planting.
219	Soil: mix with well-rotted compost or farmyard manure and apply in the root zone.
220	Drip: some liquid products can go through drip; follow the label.
221	Always at the dose and method on the label.
222	Why biofertilizers sometimes fail
223	Old or badly stored product. Keep packs in a cool, shaded place, never in the sun or a hot vehicle, and use before expiry.
224	Mixed with chemical seed treatment or fungicide at the same time. Apply them separately.
225	Dry soil. Microbes need moisture to settle in.
226	Soil with almost no organic matter, where they have nothing to live on.
227	Wrong match: Rhizobium on a non-legume does nothing.
228	What to expect
229	Biofertilizers support nutrient supply and root health over a season, and can let many growers reduce chemical fertilizer. They do not replace all fertilizer at once, and results build over seasons with organic matter. Use products approved under the Fertiliser Control Order. See <a href="/guides/soil-microbial-activity">soil microbial activity</a> and <a href="/guides/reduce-chemical-fertilizer-without-yield-loss">reduce chemical fertilizer without losing yield</a>.
230	<a href="https://ncof.dacnet.nic.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">National Centre for Organic and Natural Farming</a>
231	Which biofertilizer is best?
232	There is no single best. Choose by crop and job: Rhizobium for pulses, Azotobacter or Azospirillum for cereals and vegetables, Acetobacter for sugarcane, PSB for phosphorus, KMB for potash, ZSB for zinc.
233	Can biofertilizers replace chemical fertilizers?
234	Not fully at once. They help the crop get more from soil and fertilizer, so many growers can reduce chemical use step by step.
235	Can I mix biofertilizer with fungicide seed treatment?
236	Not at the same time. Apply them separately, as the label says, because fungicides can kill the microbes.
237	How should biofertilizers be stored?
238	In a cool, shaded place, away from sun and heat, and used before the expiry date.
### /guides/reduce-chemical-fertilizer-without-yield-loss
239	How to Reduce Chemical Fertilizer Without Reducing Yield
240	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / How to reduce chemical fertilizer without...
241	How to reduce chemical fertilizer without losing yield: a step-by-step path
242	Many farms in Gujarat apply phosphorus every year to soil already rich in it. Cutting chemical fertilizer starts with cutting what the crop never uses, not what it needs.
243	Cut waste first, not the crop's food. Test the soil to stop applying what is already there, place and split fertilizer so more reaches the roots, build organic matter, and add biofertilizers. Reduce in steps over seasons, comparing a strip of the field each time, rather than stopping all fertilizer at once.
244	Why cutting all at once fails
245	A field used to high chemical doses, with low organic matter, cannot feed a crop on its own in one season. Growers who stop suddenly often lose yield and go back. Growers who reduce step by step, while building the soil, usually keep their yield and lower their cost.
246	The path, step by step
247	Season 1
248	Soil test; stop applying nutrients the test shows are high; place fertilizer near roots; split nitrogen
249	Season 1 onward
250	Add compost or farmyard manure; return crop residues; add biofertilizers suited to the crop
251	Season 2
252	Reduce chemical NPK by a modest share on one test strip; compare yield with the rest of the field
253	Season 3 onward
254	If the test strip held its yield, extend the reduction; test the soil again
255	Where most fertilizer is wasted
256	Applying phosphorus every year on soils already high in it.
257	Broadcasting on the surface instead of placing near roots.
258	Giving all the nitrogen at once, much of it lost before the crop can use it.
259	Fertilizer on dry soil, or just before heavy rain.
260	Ignoring zinc, sulphur or boron, so the NPK cannot do its job.
261	Tools that help
262	Soil and leaf tests. See <a href="/guides/how-to-take-a-soil-sample">how to take a soil sample</a>.
263	Compost and humic substances. See <a href="/guides/compost-and-humic-acid">compost and humic acid</a>.
264	Biofertilizers. See <a href="/guides/which-biofertilizers-to-use">which biofertilizers to use</a>.
265	Legumes and green manure in the rotation.
266	Drip fertigation, which puts small amounts where the roots are.
267	When DAP is short: <a href="/guides/dap-shortage-what-to-use-instead">what to use instead of DAP</a>.
268	<a href="https://soilhealth.dac.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Soil Health Card, Government of India</a>
269	How can I reduce chemical fertilizer without losing yield?
270	Test the soil, stop applying what is already high, place and split fertilizer, build organic matter, add biofertilizers, and reduce in steps on a test strip before the whole field.
271	Can I stop chemical fertilizer completely?
272	Stopping suddenly usually lowers yield. Reducing step by step over seasons, while building organic matter, is safer.
273	How much fertilizer can I save?
274	It depends on your soil and how much was being wasted. A soil test and a test strip in your own field are the honest way to find out.

275	Organic Farming vs Natural Farming: What Is the Difference?
276	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Organic farming vs natural farming
277	Organic farming vs natural farming: the difference, the rules and which suits you
278	Organic and natural farming both say no to chemicals, but they are not the same. One can earn a certified price premium; the other aims to cut what you buy. Choosing the wrong one for your market costs years.
279	Both avoid synthetic chemicals. Organic farming allows bought organic inputs such as compost, manures, biofertilizers and approved products, and can be certified (NPOP for export, PGS-India for the domestic market) after a conversion period. Natural farming relies mainly on on-farm inputs such as cow dung and urine preparations, mulching and mixed cropping, with minimal bought inputs, and is promoted by the government's National Mission on Natural Farming.
280	Organic farming
281	Natural farming
282	Synthetic fertilizers and pesticides
283	Not allowed
284	Not allowed
285	Bought inputs
286	Allowed if approved for organic use: compost, manures, biofertilizers, bio-pesticides
287	Kept to a minimum; mostly made on the farm
288	Typical inputs
289	Compost, vermicompost, FYM, biofertilizers, green manure
290	Jeevamrut, beejamrut, mulch, mixed crops, desi cow based preparations
291	Certification
292	NPOP (third party, for export) or PGS-India (group based, domestic)
293	Not required; some states support group recognition
294	Conversion period
295	Usually 2 years for seasonal crops and 3 years for perennial crops before certified sale
296	No formal period
297	Market
298	Certified organic price premium where buyers exist
299	Mostly local and direct sales
300	What both share
301	Building soil organic matter and soil life.
302	Mulching and keeping soil covered.
303	Crop rotation, intercropping and legumes.
304	Biological pest management.
305	Patience: yields often dip in the first seasons and recover as the soil improves.
306	If you want to sell certified produce, especially for export, choose organic and plan certification from the start.
307	If you want to cut input cost and sell locally, natural farming practices can fit.
308	Many growers start with one plot, not the whole farm, and expand what works.
309	Either way, keep records of what you apply and what you harvest.

314	What is the difference between organic and natural farming?

324	Biological pest and disease control: what works, and how to use it well
325	A single Trichogramma card holds thousands of tiny wasps that hunt borer eggs before they hatch. Biological control is not weaker than chemicals; it is earlier.
326	Biological control uses living organisms and natural products to keep pests and diseases below damaging levels: beneficial fungi and bacteria such as Trichoderma, Pseudomonas, Beauveria and Metarhizium, viruses such as NPV, parasitoids such as Trichogramma, and traps and neem. They work best early and preventively, as part of integrated pest management, not as a last rescue.

357	Rules that make them work
358	Start early. Biologicals prevent and slow pests; they rarely rescue a crop that is already badly hit.
359	Fungal products need humidity; spray in the evening.
360	Do not mix live fungal or bacterial products with chemical fungicides in the same tank.
361	Use fresh product from a registered source, stored cool, before expiry. Bio-pesticides must be registered with the Central Insecticides Board.
362	Count pests with traps and field checks, so you act at the right time.
363	Protect natural enemies: ladybirds, spiders and lacewings are free pest control. Avoid broad-spectrum sprays where you can.
364	Prevention first
365	Healthy seed and seed treatment.
366	Crop rotation, and removing infected plants and residues.
367	Balanced nutrition: too much nitrogen invites sucking pests.
368	Good drainage and spacing so leaves dry quickly.
369	Strong roots and healthy soil; see <a href="/guides/soil-microbial-activity">soil microbial activity</a>.
370	When to use chemicals
371	When pests cross the level that causes economic loss and biological options cannot hold them, a registered chemical at the label dose may be needed. Choose the most specific product, and leave a gap before returning to biologicals. 373	What is biological pest control?
374	Using living organisms and natural products, such as beneficial fungi, bacteria, viruses, parasitic wasps, traps and neem, to keep pests and diseases below damaging levels.
375	Does Trichoderma control root rot?
376	Trichoderma is widely used as a seed and soil treatment to protect roots against root rot, wilt and damping off. It works best as prevention.
377	Can I mix bio-pesticides with chemical fungicides?
378	Do not mix live fungal or bacterial products with chemical fungicides in the same tank; the fungicide can kill them.
379	Do biological controls work as fast as chemicals?
380	Usually slower. They work best early and as prevention, combined with traps, monitoring and good crop care.
