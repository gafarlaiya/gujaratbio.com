8	Mango Flowering Spray Schedule and Care: Kesar, Nov to Fruit Set
9	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Mango flowering care and spray schedule
10	Mango flowering care and spray schedule: more flowers, protected, from November to fruit set
11	Mango flowering spray schedule, stage by stage
12	There is no single magic flowering spray. Each stage has one job, and most orchards need only two or three well-timed rounds. The options below are those listed in the Government of India's integrated pest management package for mango.
13	Job
14	Options listed for mango
15	November to December, before panicles
16	Mature the shoots, stop leafy flush
17	Usually no spray is needed. Correct zinc or boron only if a leaf or soil test shows a shortage (zinc sulphate, borax).
18	Panicle emergence
19	Protect from hoppers and powdery mildew
20	Hoppers: imidacloprid, buprofezin, lambda-cyhalothrin or deltamethrin; biological: Beauveria bassiana or Metarhizium anisopliae. Powdery mildew: wettable sulphur, hexaconazole, penconazole or azoxystrobin.
21	Cloudy, humid or drizzly spell
22	Protect panicles from blossom blight (anthracnose)
23	Copper oxychloride or azoxystrobin.
24	2 to 3 weeks later, before flowers open
25	Second round, only if hoppers or mildew are present
26	Use a different chemical group from the first round.
27	Pollination
28	No sprays.
29	Pea stage
30	Hold fruit, prevent black tip
31	Borax at pea stage, as in the IPM package; water at regular intervals. Ask your KVK before any fruit-drop growth regulator.
32	We do not print doses on this page. The right dose depends on the product's strength, its formulation and the size of the tree, and doses copied from the internet are often wrong. Use the dose on the label of the product you buy, registered for mango, or your state agricultural university's recommendation.
33	Spray in the early morning or evening, never in the midday heat.
34	Mix at most one insecticide and one fungicide in a tank, and only if the labels say they mix.
35	Rotate chemical groups between rounds so hoppers and mildew do not become resistant.
36	Cover the whole panicle and the inner canopy; hoppers hide inside.
37	Does potassium nitrate increase mango flowering?
38	Potassium nitrate sprays are best known from warm tropical mango areas, where winters are not cold enough to trigger flowering. In Gujarat, where cool winter nights already do that job, results are mixed, and it is not part of the national IPM package for mango. It cannot make immature shoots flower. If you want to try it, follow your state university's recommendation, spray a few trees, and keep a few unsprayed to compare.
39	What is the best spray for mango flowering?
40	There is no single best spray. At panicle emergence, protect against hoppers and powdery mildew, repeat only if needed before flowers open, and do not spray at full bloom. Use products registered for mango at the label dose.
41	Does potassium nitrate spray increase mango flowering?
42	It is best known from warm tropical areas. In Gujarat's cooler winters results are mixed. It cannot make immature shoots flower. Test it on a few trees first.
43	Can I spray mango trees during flowering?
44	Not at full bloom. Spray at panicle emergence and before flowers open. Spraying open flowers harms bees and flies and can burn the flowers.

45	Sugarcane Ratoon Management After Harvest: Step by Step
46	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Sugarcane ratoon management after harvest
47	Sugarcane ratoon management after harvest: the first 45 days decide the crop
48	A ratoon is the cheapest sugarcane you will ever grow: no setts, no planting. But the stubble does not care about your savings. Leave it high, burn the trash and wait a month to irrigate, and the cheap crop becomes the poor one.
49	A good ratoon is made in the first four to six weeks after harvest. Cut stubble at ground level, spread the trash between rows instead of burning it, loosen the soil beside the rows, irrigate, fill gaps early with pre-sprouted settlings, and feed the ratoon a little more nitrogen than the plant crop got.
50	the window after harvest that sets the ratoon
51	fires: trash belongs between the rows
52	Why ratoons fall behind
53	Stubble left high sprouts from upper buds, which give weak, shallow-rooted shoots that lodge later.
54	Gaps from the plant crop stay gaps, and grow with every ratoon.
55	The soil between rows is hard and packed after a year of traffic and harvest.
56	Burnt trash leaves the soil bare, dry and short of organic matter.
57	Cane cut in the coldest weeks sprouts slowly; buds wait for warmth.
58	Step by step after the last cane is cut
59	How
60	Day 0 to 3
61	Stubble shaving
62	Cut any stubble left above ground with a sharp tool, at or just below soil level, so new shoots come from the lower buds.
63	Day 0 to 7
64	Trash, not fire
65	Spread the dry leaves between the rows as mulch. Pull it off the row itself so shoots are not smothered.
66	Week 1
67	Loosen the sides (off-barring)
68	Loosen the soil on both sides of the row with a plough or cultivator. This cuts old roots and lets new roots and water in.
69	Week 1
70	First irrigation
71	Irrigate soon after shaving and loosening. Dry soil after harvest is a common reason for poor sprouting.
72	Week 1 to 2
73	Basal feeding
74	Apply the basal dose for the ratoon near the row. Ratoons need more nitrogen than the plant crop.
75	Week 3 to 5
76	Gap filling
77	Walk the rows. Fill gaps longer than about 60 cm with pre-sprouted settlings from a nursery or polybags, and water them in.
78	Day 45 onward
79	Second feeding and weeding
80	Second nitrogen dose and weeding; then follow your normal tillering-stage care.
81	Timings are a general guide; follow your state university or sugar factory recommendation for doses.
82	Why trash is worth more than the fire
83	A hectare of sugarcane leaves several tonnes of dry trash. Burnt, it is gone in an hour. Spread between rows, it keeps moisture in, keeps weeds down, protects soil life, and slowly returns organic matter. Many growers speed it up with a trash decomposer culture and a little water. If you must remove some trash, do not burn the rest on the field.
84	Feeding the ratoon
85	A ratoon grows from an old root system in packed soil, so it needs more nitrogen than the plant crop. Follow the ratoon dose in your state or factory recommendation.
86	Put fertilizer close to the row after loosening the soil, not on top of the trash.
87	Acetobacter and other nitrogen-fixing bacteria can support the ratoon's nitrogen supply alongside fertilizer. See <a href="/products/immunity-acetobacter">Immunity Acetobacter</a>.
88	Organic matter and a living soil help the old stubble root again. See <a href="/products/immunity-sugarcane-special">Immunity Sugarcane Special</a> and the <a href="/crops/sugarcane">sugarcane crop guide</a>.
89	How many ratoons to keep
90	Keep a ratoon only when the plant crop was healthy and gaps are few.
91	If more than about a fifth of the row is gaps, or red rot, wilt or smut was serious, replant instead.
92	Most growers in Gujarat keep one or two ratoons. Each ratoon is cheaper to grow but usually yields less, unless managed well.
93	Related
94	<a href="/guides/how-to-increase-sugarcane-tillers">How to increase sugarcane tillers</a> and <a href="/crop-problems/sugarcane-poor-germination">sugarcane poor germination and gaps</a>.
95	<a href="https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/expert_system/sugar/ratoon.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">TNAU Agritech: sugarcane ratoon management</a>
96	<a href="https://sugarcane.icar.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ICAR-Sugarcane Breeding Institute</a>
97	What should I do after harvesting sugarcane for a ratoon?
98	Shave stubble to ground level, spread the trash between rows, loosen the soil beside the rows, irrigate, apply the ratoon basal dose, and fill gaps within about a month.
99	Should I burn sugarcane trash?
100	No. Spread it between rows as mulch. It keeps moisture, controls weeds and returns organic matter. Burning wastes it and leaves the soil bare.
101	When should gaps be filled in a ratoon?
102	Within about three to five weeks of harvest, with pre-sprouted settlings, so they keep pace with the ratoon.
103	Does a ratoon need more fertilizer?
104	It needs more nitrogen than the plant crop, because it grows from an old root system in packed soil. Follow your state or factory recommendation.
105	Why is my ratoon sprouting poorly?
106	Common reasons are high stubble, dry soil after harvest, hard packed soil, burnt trash, cold weather at harvest, or gaps left from the plant crop.

107	How to Increase Sugarcane Tillers, and Keep Them Alive
108	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Sugarcane tillers
109	Sugarcane tillers: how to increase them, and why only some become cane
110	A sugarcane clump can push out far more tillers than will ever reach the mill. Many die quietly in the shade. The yield is decided not by how many tillers you see in month three, but by how many strong ones are still standing at harvest.
111	Sugarcane tillers form mostly in the first three to four months after planting or ratooning. Light reaching the base of the plant, steady moisture, enough nitrogen and loose, airy soil make more tillers. Many tillers die later; the aim is not the most tillers but the most strong tillers that survive to harvest.
112	3-4 months
113	when tillering peaks
114	earthing up, after tillering, not before
115	How tillering works
116	Each bud that sprouts gives a mother shoot. From its underground nodes come the tillers, then tillers of tillers. Tillering peaks around three to four months after planting. After that the canopy closes, the clump competes for light, and the weakest, latest tillers die. Only the earlier, thicker tillers usually grow into millable cane.
117	What makes a clump tiller well
118	Light at the base. Wide rows and early weed control let light reach the soil, which wakes up tiller buds.
119	Steady moisture. Long dry gaps in the tillering months cut tiller numbers.
120	Nitrogen on time. Split nitrogen so enough is there during the tillering months, not all at once.
121	Loose, airy soil around the clump. Hard or waterlogged soil gives few, weak tillers.
122	Healthy setts and an even start. Gaps and late shoots never catch up.
123	Earthing up too early. Full earthing up stops new tillers, so it is done after tillering, not during it.
124	Weeds in the first three months, which steal light and nitrogen exactly when tillers form.
125	Heavy late nitrogen after tillering, which pushes late tillers that die anyway.
126	Very close planting, which shades the base and raises tiller death.
127	Count your tillers
128	Around 90 to 100 days, count shoots per clump on a few metres of row in good and weak patches.
129	A low count in one patch usually points to gaps, dry or hard soil, or weeds there.
130	Record it in your farm diary, so you can compare this crop with the next. The Hariyo app has a tiller count for each field.
131	If counts are low across the field, ask us with photos and your planting date.
132	Where biological inputs fit
133	Nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Acetobacter and a living, organic soil support steady growth in the tillering months, alongside fertilizer. See <a href="/products/immunity-sugarcane-special">Immunity Sugarcane Special</a>, <a href="/guides/sugarcane-ratoon-management">sugarcane ratoon management</a> and the <a href="/crops/sugarcane">sugarcane crop guide</a>.
134	<a href="https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/expert_system/sugar/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">TNAU Agritech: sugarcane expert system</a>
135	<a href="https://iisr.icar.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ICAR-Indian Institute of Sugarcane Research</a>
136	When does sugarcane tillering happen?
137	Mostly in the first three to four months after planting or ratooning, peaking around three to four months.
138	How can I increase tillers in sugarcane?
139	Control weeds early, keep moisture steady, give nitrogen on time during the tillering months, keep the soil loose and let light reach the base. Do not earth up fully until tillering is over.
140	Why do sugarcane tillers die?
141	After the canopy closes, late and weak tillers lose the competition for light, water and nutrients. Some tiller death is normal.
142	Does earthing up stop tillering?
143	Yes. Full earthing up covers the tiller buds, so it is done after the tillering phase.
### /guides/salty-borewell-water-for-crops
144	Salty Borewell Water: Effect on Crops and What to Do
145	<a href="/">Home</a> / <a href="/guides">Guides</a> / Salty borewell water
146	Salty borewell water: how it hurts crops, how to test it, and how to farm with it
147	Your field can be wet and your crop still thirsty. When borewell water is salty, the salt holds the water back from the roots, and every irrigation leaves a little more salt behind.
148	Salty borewell water leaves salt in the root zone, so roots struggle to take up water even in wet soil. Leaf edges burn, germination is poor and yields fall. Test the water's EC once. Below about 0.7 dS/m it is generally safe; above about 3 dS/m it needs careful management, salt-tolerant crops and good drainage.
149	0.7 dS/m
150	below this, water is safe for most crops
151	3 dS/m
152	above this, severe restriction
153	What salt does to a crop
154	Salt in the soil water holds water back from the roots, so the crop shows thirst even after irrigation.
155	Leaf tips and edges dry and burn, starting on older leaves.
156	Germination falls and seedlings die, because salt collects near the surface where the seed is.
157	Some waters also carry a lot of sodium or bicarbonate. Over years this makes the soil hard, sticky and slow to drain.
158	Test the water, once a year
159	Take a clean bottle of water after the pump has run for a few minutes and ask a soil and water testing lab for EC, pH, and RSC (residual sodium carbonate). Many labs also report TDS; as a rough guide, TDS in mg/L is about EC in dS/m multiplied by 640.
160	TDS (mg/L)
161	Below about 450
162	No restriction for most crops
163	About 450 to 2,000
164	Slight to moderate restriction: choose crops and manage carefully
165	Above about 2,000
166	Severe restriction: tolerant crops, good drainage and blending only
167	Bands from the FAO guidelines on water quality for agriculture. Soil type, rainfall and drainage change how much a given water hurts; light soils with good rain cope better than heavy, poorly drained soils.
168	Which crops cope better
169	More tolerant
170	Barley, cotton, sugar beet, date palm
171	Moderately tolerant
172	Wheat, mustard, sorghum
173	Moderately sensitive
174	Sugarcane, groundnut, maize, tomato, chilli, potato
175	Sensitive
176	Onion, beans and most pulses, mango, citrus, carrot
177	Varieties differ; ask your KVK for salt-tolerant varieties of your crop. Seedlings are more sensitive than established plants.
178	How to keep farming with salty water
179	Blend or alternate: use canal or rain-harvested water for germination and early growth, and the borewell later.
180	Irrigate lightly and more often. Letting the soil dry out concentrates salt around the roots.
181	Use drip where you can. It keeps the root zone moist and pushes salt to the edge of the wetted area.
182	On ridges and beds, sow on the side of the ridge, not the top, where salt collects.
183	Let the monsoon flush salt down: keep fields level and bunded so rain soaks in, and keep drains open.
184	Add organic matter: compost and crop residues improve structure so salt can be washed below the roots.
185	Gypsum helps where the problem is sodium or high RSC, not plain salt. Use it on a lab's advice.
186	Mulch the soil surface to cut evaporation, which pulls salt up.
187	Some seaweed, amino acid and humic products are used to help crops cope with salt stress. They do not remove salt, and they work only alongside the water and soil steps above. See <a href="/guides/biostimulants-for-salinity-and-drought-stress">biostimulants for salinity and drought stress</a> and <a href="/guides/why-yields-fall-on-old-land">why yields fall on old land</a>.
188	<a href="https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/t0234e00.htm" rel="noopener" target="_blank">FAO: Water quality for agriculture (Irrigation and Drainage Paper 29)</a>
189	<a href="https://cssri.res.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ICAR-Central Soil Salinity Research Institute</a>
190	How do I know if my borewell water is salty?
191	Test it for EC in a soil and water testing lab. Burnt leaf edges, white crust on the soil and poor germination are signs, but only a test tells you how salty.
192	What EC of water is good for irrigation?
193	Below about 0.7 dS/m is safe for most crops. From 0.7 to 3 dS/m needs care; above 3 dS/m is severe.
194	What is a safe TDS for irrigation water?
195	Below about 450 mg/L is generally safe. Above about 2,000 mg/L severely limits most crops.
196	Can salty water be used for drip irrigation?
197	Yes, often more safely than flood irrigation, because drip keeps the root zone moist. Flush the lines and let the monsoon wash salt down.
198	Does gypsum remove salt from water?
199	No. Gypsum helps where the water or soil has too much sodium or bicarbonate. It does not remove ordinary salt.
200	Which crops grow well with salty water?
201	Barley, cotton and sugar beet are among the more tolerant; wheat and mustard moderately. Onion, pulses, mango and citrus are sensitive.
