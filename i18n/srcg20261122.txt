107	Why many farms can do without DAP for a season
108	Only about 15 to 25% of the phosphorus in fertilizer becomes available to the crop. The rest stays in the soil, locked with calcium in alkaline soils or with iron and aluminium in acid soils. After 20 or 30 years of DAP, most old farms hold a large bank of this phosphorus.
109	So the real question is not only where to buy the next bag. It is how to get the crop's roots to the phosphorus you have already paid for. That needs a loose, living soil and a big, healthy root system. This is what the Immunity program is made for. The science is explained in <a href="/guides/free-locked-phosphorus-in-soil">how to free locked phosphorus</a>.

255	India's National Academy of Agricultural Sciences reports that only 15 to 25% of fertilizer phosphorus becomes available to plants, and only 1 to 3% of all the phosphorus in a soil is available at any time. In alkaline soils, above pH 7, phosphorus reacts with calcium and magnesium. In acid soils, below pH 6, it reacts with iron and aluminium. Either way it turns into forms that roots cannot easily take up.
256	Much of Gujarat has alkaline, calcium-rich black and coastal soils. Here a large share of each DAP bag ends up as calcium phosphate. It is not lost; it is locked. And after decades of DAP, many fields hold a large bank of it.
257	Signs your soil is holding phosphorus back
258	The soil test shows medium or high phosphorus, but the crop still shows phosphorus hunger: slow early growth, weak roots, a purple tinge on young wheat or maize leaves.
259	Yields have stopped rising though you use more DAP every year.

263	<strong>Humic substances.</strong> In a study on alkaline soil (Wang and others, Soil Use and Management, 1995), adding humic acids with phosphorus fertilizer increased water-soluble phosphate, slowed the formation of locked forms, and raised phosphorus uptake and wheat yield by 25%. 264	<strong>Microbes that dissolve phosphate.</strong> Phosphate-solubilising bacteria and fungi release organic acids that dissolve calcium phosphate near the roots. The national academy recommends such consortia, with mycorrhiza, to raise phosphorus use. See <a href="/products/immunity-psb">Immunity PSB</a>, <a href="/products/immunity-psf">PSF</a> and <a href="/products/immunity-mycorrhiza">Mycorrhiza</a>.
265	<strong>Organic matter.</strong> Compost and well-rotted farmyard manure release phosphorus slowly and keep it in forms roots can use. See <a href="/guides/compost-and-humic-acid">compost and humic acid</a>.
266	<strong>Bigger roots.</strong> Phosphorus barely moves, so the roots must come to it. A larger, deeper root system, extended by mycorrhiza, reaches far more of the soil.
267	<strong>Placement.</strong> Phosphorus and products placed near the seed or in the root zone work better than the same amount spread on top.
268	A softer soil, deeper roots
269	Hard, compact soil keeps roots short and shallow, so they touch only a thin layer of the phosphorus bank. When soil life and organic matter return, the soil crumbles, holds air and water, and roots travel deeper.
270	A living soil also holds nutrients better. Soil scientists call this the cation exchange capacity: the soil's power to hold calcium, magnesium, potash and other nutrients like a sponge and release them to the roots. Organic matter raises it. And around every active root, the acids made by roots and microbes lower the pH in a thin zone, where locked calcium phosphate dissolves even when the rest of the soil stays alkaline.

304	Where your urea goes
305	In India, heavy urea use has pulled nitrogen use efficiency down to about 30 to 45%. Most of the rest escapes as gas from urea left on the soil surface, or washes below the roots with irrigation water. Too much urea also makes crops soft and lush, so pests and diseases come faster and the crop can fall over.
306	So every bag saved from loss is a bag you do not need to buy. When urea, DAP or potash is short, that matters twice.
307	Use fewer bags of urea
308	Split urea into two or three doses at the stages the crop needs it, instead of one heavy dose.
309	Give it just before irrigation, or mix it into the soil. Urea left on dry, hot soil turns to gas.
310	Do not overdo early doses. Watch the leaf colour, and add more only if the crop turns pale.
311	Use nitrogen-fixing bacteria: <a href="/products/immunity-azotobacter">Azotobacter</a> for wheat and vegetables, <a href="/products/immunity-azospirillum">Azospirillum</a> for cereals, <a href="/products/immunity-acetobacter">Acetobacter</a> for sugarcane and <a href="/products/immunity-rhizobium">Rhizobium</a> for pulses.
